Good afternoon. Today's lecture is about how lighthouses learned to see further, and the answer is mostly one invention, the Fresnel lens. Before eighteen twenty, a lighthouse used a large curved mirror, called a parabolic reflector, behind an oil lamp. Reflectors were heavy, they tarnished, and they wasted more than half of the light. Augustin Fresnel, a French engineer, realised that a lens only bends light at its surfaces, so the thick glass in the middle of a large lens does nothing useful except absorb light. His solution was to cut the lens into concentric rings, each ring keeping only the curved surface it needed. The result was a lens that was thin, light, and could be made enormous.
Fresnel added a second idea. Above and below the central rings he placed prisms that caught light escaping upwards and downwards and redirected it toward the horizon. This is called catadioptric design, because it uses both refraction, the bending of light through glass, and total internal reflection inside the prisms. A first order Fresnel lens could send a beam more than twenty nautical miles, roughly four times the range of the best reflector.
The lenses were graded by order. A first order lens, the largest, stood taller than a person and was used on major sea coasts. Sixth order lenses were small enough to carry, and marked harbours and rivers. Each lighthouse was also given a characteristic, a pattern of flashes and pauses, so that a sailor could tell which light he was looking at. The flashes were produced by rotating panels of the lens around the lamp, and the whole assembly often floated in a bath of mercury so that a clockwork motor could turn several tonnes of glass with very little friction.
The main argument I want you to take away is this. The Fresnel lens did not make light brighter. It made light less wasted. Almost every improvement in lighthouse range for the next century came from collecting and aiming light more efficiently, not from a stronger flame.

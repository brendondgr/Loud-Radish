Speech model

Language

A two-letter code. Setting it explicitly is more reliable than detection, which occasionally switches language mid-talk on a technical term.

1 is greedy and fastest. Higher is more accurate and proportionally slower — raise it only if the real-time factor has room.

Terms and names

Paste the speaker's name, the venue, and any jargon before the talk. The model is biased toward these spellings, which is the single most effective thing available for a seminar full of proper nouns it has never seen.

Gives the model continuity across buffer boundaries. Occasionally lets a mistake reinforce itself, so turn it off if the same error keeps recurring.

Invented speech

Speech models do not go quiet on silence — they produce the phrases their training data ends with, usually "thank you", "bye", or a stray word, and it looks exactly like a transcript. These discard it. Loosening them lets more of it through; tightening them risks dropping speech that was quiet, accented, or far from the microphone.

Strips silence and noise out of the audio before the model sees it. Measured as slightly faster rather than slower, because there is less left to transcribe.

likelihood of no speech

The model's own estimate that a stretch of audio contained nobody talking. 0.6 is the default; lower discards more.

Only when one of them is a short passage entirely by itself. "Thank you all for coming" is untouched, and so is a thank-you spoken slowly.

average confidence

Off at 0, which is the default. A blunt measure — it penalises quiet speakers rather than invented text specifically.

Committing text

Text is committed once consecutive passes agree on it. Committed text never changes; the tentative tail is replaced wholesale each pass. These control that trade-off between how fast text settles and how often it settles on the wrong thing.

2 is the working default. 3 commits more slowly and revises less.
